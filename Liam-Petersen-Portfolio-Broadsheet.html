SECTION A

The Read — Why One Ad Works & The Other Doesn't

By Liam Petersen · The part most teams can't explain

The mechanic behind the winners — hook, first frame, tension, proof, CTA — read the way I read every test.

Teardown No. 1 — same script, two hooks

"Why I got rid of my fabric stash" vs. "My stash needed an upgrade"

✗ KILLED

"My stash needed an UPGRADE…"

✓ RAN

"Why I got rid of my fabric stash…"

THE READ — both point at the same problem, but only one opens a loop. "Needed an upgrade" is a statement: the viewer already knows how it ends. "Why I got rid of my fabric stash" is a confession with the reason missing — it implies a story, a mistake, a decision, and the brain has to watch the next second to close it. Same product. Same body copy. The only variable is whether the first three words make you need the fourth.
Teardown No. 2 — same product, opposite buyer

The calming angle vs. the fabric-quality angle

✓ RAN — A

"My go-to relaxing hobby…"

✓ RAN — B

"Need quality fabrics? Try this…"

THE READ — this isn't A-beats-B. It's two different buyers for one box. Angle A sells escape (the woman shopping for calm, not craft); Angle B sells proof of quality (the experienced quilter tired of faded fabric). Running both at once is the whole point: you don't guess which desire the market wants — you let delivery tell you which one it can scale, then build the next batch off the winner. The mistake is picking one on taste. The job is to ship both and read the signal.

Long-Form & Landing Copy

When the click has to become a sale

Sales letters, advertorials and landing pages where the whole job is carrying a cold reader from hook to checkout. Selected leads below.

Posh PeanutLong-form sales letter — the $168 reversible blanketScene-immersion register · 13 sections+

"The blanket that refuses to choose."

Warm but never hot. Weighted but feather-light. Soft on both sides. The weight is the first thing you notice. You ordered it on a Sunday, half-talking yourself out of it the whole time, because $168 is a lot for a blanket and you already own four. It arrives folded tighter than it has any right to be. You lift it out, braced for either of the two disappointments you've learned to expect… It is neither. That is the first surprise.
WHY IT WORKS — opens on a physical contradiction (warm-but-not-hot, weighted-but-light) the reader has to keep reading to resolve, then lives inside a specific buying moment — "you ordered it on a Sunday, half-talking yourself out of it" — so the price objection gets named before it's raised.

Role: full research, positioning & long-form copy. Built to open with the objection, not hide from it.

TactacamLong-form sales letter — cellular trail cameraHunting / outdoor · $179.99 · high-intent buyer+

"The trail cam that talks after dying."

It's 5:14 in the morning. You woke up before the alarm — you always do, on this kind of morning. Now you're sitting at the kitchen table with the phone face-down beside the mug. You're going to flip it over and the app is either going to show you the buck moved across the back field at 3:42 a.m., or that your camera went silent at 9:47 p.m. and hasn't said a word since. That second screen is the one you've seen before. Twice last fall.
WHY IT WORKS — a completely different audience (male, rural, rational) gets a completely different register — cold, precise, cinematic. The lead dramatizes the exact failure the product prevents (a dead camera on opening morning) instead of listing specs, so every spec that follows lands as relief.

Role: full research, positioning & long-form copy. Range proof: same craft, opposite buyer to Posh Peanut.

Project MAngle system — perimenopause botanicalFive tested ad angles engineered from one brand+

One brand, five distinct angles built to test against each other across the funnel — category-naming, stat-led, mechanism, transformation and authority:

  • Us-vs-them: "The space between 'here's a prescription' and 'just live with it.'"
  • Stat-led: "35 women. 86% felt better in 30 days. 100% of women on HRT improved too."
  • Mechanism: "Gardenia. Dong Quai. Poria. Eight more. One formula they've used for six centuries."
  • Before/after: "Restless nights. Restored mornings."
  • Authority: "Formulated by Harvard-trained herbalists. Tested by 600 years."
WHY IT WORKS — a subtle one: "Gardenia. Dong Quai. Poria. Eight more." outperforms a closed list of all ten herbs, because the open loop ("which eight?") pulls the read-through. Five angles isn't indecision; it's a test matrix — the market picks the winner, not my taste.

Role: angle strategy, headline systems & copy. Each angle designed as a test cell, not a one-off.

— Advertisement (Spec) —
Client (illustrative): a local roofing / HVAC company · goal: lower cost-per-call

1 — Emergency

Storm / leak / breakdown. Highest intent, lowest patience. "Roof leaking after last night's storm?"

2 — Trust / local

Their real fear is the scam, the no-show, the overcharge. "The [city] crew your neighbours actually call back."

3 — Cost fear

Agitate the small problem into the big bill. "That little brown stain? A $12k repair waiting to happen."

4 — Seasonal

Pre-empt the rush. "Before the first heatwave, book the tune-up everyone forgets."

SECTION F

The Five Rules I Test By

By Liam Petersen · The system behind the copy
  • 1
    Hooks first. The hook is ~80% of a video ad's performance, so it's variable №1 — four to five hooks against one body, isolated, so I know what actually moved the number.
  • 2
    One angle ≠ one audience. The same product gets multiple emotional angles run in parallel. I don't guess which desire wins — the algorithm tells me which one it can scale.
  • 3
    Feed the machine, don't micro-target. In the Andromeda / Advantage+ era, creative is the new targeting. Broad audience, diverse creative, let delivery match — then read the winners.
  • 4
    Read the funnel, not the vanity. Hook rate judges the hook, hold rate judges the body, and CPL / cost-per-acquisition is the only number that decides what lives.
  • 5
    Diagnose, then reproduce. When one ad wins, name why — the mechanic, not the luck — and rebuild the next batch off it. That's how a winner becomes a system instead of an accident.
